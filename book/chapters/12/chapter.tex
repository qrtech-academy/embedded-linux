\chapter{Real-Time Linux}
\label{c12:ch}

{\large\itshape What all of it costs in latency, and what a real-time kernel changes about the
answer.\par}

\begin{chapterbox}{In this chapter}
\begin{itemize}
  \item What ``real time'' claims, and why it is a statement about the worst case and never the
    average.
  \item Three sources of latency, only one of which is the scheduler.
  \item Priority inversion, and the spacecraft it kept resetting.
  \item Four preemption models, and what each one is willing to interrupt.
  \item What \code{PREEMPT_RT} actually changes: sleeping spinlocks, threaded interrupts,
    inheritance.
  \item Which locks do not change, and why \code{raw_spinlock_t} exists.
  \item \code{cyclictest}: what it measures and what it structurally cannot see.
  \item Measure your own driver's latency both ways, interrupt to handler and handler to
    userspace, and plot it.
\end{itemize}
\end{chapterbox}

This chapter is about what everything the book has built costs in latency, and what a real-time
kernel changes about the answer. It defines real time by the worst case rather than the average,
names the three places latency comes from, and tells the Mars Pathfinder story as it happened. It
sets out the four preemption models, what \code{PREEMPT_RT}, merged in the 6.12 kernel this course
builds, does to spinlocks, interrupt handlers and mutexes, and which locks it leaves alone. It then
turns to measurement: what \code{cyclictest} measures and what it cannot, how a device that
timestamps its own interrupt lets latency be subtracted rather than inferred, and why a number taken
under an emulator is worthless on its own while the comparison between two kernels survives.

Read \secref{c12:app:a} first; it is the theory. \Secref{c12:app:b} is the measurement, and the lab
is a measurement too: Exercise~\ref{c12:ex:6} specifies a driver that timestamps every interrupt
twice and delivers the record declared in the header printed in \secref{c12:app:b3}, and the course
ships the program that reads it. The second kernel is \code{make kernel RT=1}, a full build of its
own, and it needs an initramfs of its own, \code{make rootfs RT=1}. The exercises in
\secref{c12:sec:exercises} end with the Cross-check that closes the course: predict whether
\code{PREEMPT_RT} lowers the mean and the maximum, measure both kernels idle and under load, and
find that the two answers differ; then state at length what the measurement is blind to, which is
the deliverable rather than the number. The lab is done when \code{make test L=L12} reports
\textbf{PASSED} with ten checks on the \code{PREEMPT} kernel, and \code{RT=1 make test L=L12} does
the same on the \code{PREEMPT_RT} one.
